Is a Real Estate Commission Rebate Legal in Canada?

By Tanya Nabein
REALTOR® · Real Estate Cash Rebate, a service of Relocation Services Group

If you have heard that a brokerage can hand part of its commission back to you and wondered whether that is really allowed, you are not alone. The short answer is yes: a real estate commission rebate is legal across Canada when it is agreed and documented in writing. Here is how it works, what the regulators in each province expect, and the simple steps that let you accept a rebate with full confidence.

Is a commission rebate actually legal in Canada?

Yes. A real estate commission rebate, where your brokerage returns part of its commission to you as the buyer or seller, is legal across Canada when it is documented in writing. No law fixes commission rates in this country, so they are negotiable everywhere, and a rebate is simply one way that negotiation shows up. The common thread among provincial regulators is straightforward: the rebate must be agreed in writing and disclosed honestly to everyone involved.

What do the rules say in Ontario?

In Ontario, real estate is governed by the Trust in Real Estate Services Act (TRESA), with its current rules in force since December 1, 2023, and overseen by the Real Estate Council of Ontario (RECO). RECO confirms that a rebate may be provided to a brokerage’s client or customer who is a party to the trade. It must be set out in writing, and any advertising of that rebate has to state the amount clearly, when it is paid, and any conditions attached. In plain terms, what you are promised should be written down and easy to understand.

Does it work the same way in BC, Alberta, and Quebec?

The principle holds across the country, with local wording in each province. In British Columbia, the BC Financial Services Authority (BCFSA) requires remuneration arrangements to be disclosed to you in writing. In Alberta, the Real Estate Council of Alberta (RECA) treats incentives as permitted but expects the details to be captured in a signed, written statement. In Quebec, the OACIQ governs broker remuneration through formal written brokerage contracts, so the rebate lives inside the paperwork you already sign. Because the details vary by province, it is always worth confirming the current rules where you are buying or selling.

Is paying me a finder’s fee for a referral the same thing?

No, and this is an important line to understand. A rebate goes to you as a party to your own transaction, which is exactly why it is permitted. A referral reward or fee for sending business is a different matter: those payments can only flow between registered brokerages. Paying an unregistered person a finder’s fee for trading activity is not allowed, and RECO is explicit that no form of compensation may go to an unregistered third party for helping along a trade. The rebate you receive on your own purchase or sale does not run into this rule.

What should I get in writing?

Get the rebate amount, or the formula used to calculate it, along with when it will be paid and any conditions, recorded in your representation or commission agreement before you proceed. A clear written record protects everyone if a question ever comes up later. Honest, accurate advertising is also required by the regulators, so what you were promised verbally should match what appears on the page. If anything is unclear, ask for it to be spelled out before you sign.

How do I check that a brokerage is legitimate?

Use your provincial regulator’s public registrant search, which is free and takes only a minute. In Ontario, RECO’s tool at registrantsearch.reco.on.ca lets you confirm that an agent or brokerage is registered and in good standing, including any recent enforcement history. British Columbia, Alberta, and Quebec all maintain similar public registers through BCFSA, RECA, and the OACIQ. A quick search before you sign is the simplest way to buy or sell with peace of mind.

Are there any tax considerations?

There can be, depending on your situation. Regulators note that rebates may carry tax implications, and a home you live in is generally treated differently than an investment or rental property. For most buyers of a principal residence this is rarely a concern, but it is worth knowing the distinction exists. We can point you in the right direction, and for your specific circumstances it is wise to confirm the details with the Canada Revenue Agency or a tax professional.

The bottom line

A commission rebate is a legal, well understood part of Canadian real estate when it is agreed in writing and disclosed openly. Lead with finding a vetted, registered agent who will do excellent work for you, then treat the cash back as a welcome bonus on top of that. Get the terms in writing, confirm the brokerage on your provincial register, and you can move forward with real confidence.
